Most people who walk into Kerem HaTeimanim for the first time are looking for lunch. The quarter sits alongside Carmel Market, and its lanes are where the Yemenite cooking that gave the market its first flavours is still served by the plate rather than sold by the kilo.

This article is about those tables: the soup houses and small family restaurants that journalists have written about over the past decade and a half, what they serve, and how to approach them as a visitor. It is not a guide to the neighbourhood itself — for the streets, the beach and the practical side, see our [guide to the Yemenite Quarter](/blog/kerem-hateimanim-neighborhood-guide).

One caution runs through everything below. The press sources we rely on date from **2012, 2016 and 2018**. Small family restaurants change hands, move, shorten their hours or close. We have not confirmed that any of the places named here is open today, and we give no hours, prices or menus as current. Check before you go.

## Why you can still eat Yemenite in the Kerem

Yemenite food has spread across Israel. The Kerem is different because the cooking never left the place where it arrived. In 2012, the Times of Israel described two of its soup houses as run by local Yemenite families; in 2018, Time Out described one as run by the daughter of its late founder.

The [Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/when-time-stops-in-the-kerem/), in a 2012 piece by Michal Shmulovich, described the quarter as a place that had changed less than the city around it: quiet, winding alleys sitting between the traffic of Allenby Street and the noise of the market, low houses with plants on the balconies, and many residents descended from the original families. It opens with the line "There is a neighborhood in Tel Aviv in which time stands still." That continuity is the reason the restaurants matter. They are not themed recreations of Yemenite food; they are what the neighbourhood has always eaten.

The dishes and condiments themselves — jachnun, malawach, samneh, hilbeh and s'chug — are explained in our article on [how Carmel Market began](/blog/carmel-market-history-from-kerem-stalls), so we will not define them again here.

## Yemenite soup, the signature dish

If there is one thing to order in the Kerem, it is soup. **Marak teimani** — Yemenite soup — is the dish the quarter's restaurants are built around, and it is less heavy than visitors expect.

The Times of Israel piece describes it as potatoes with beef, chicken or both, in a light but spicy broth. The soup arrives plain; you season it yourself at the table with the condiments set out beside it — a spoon of hilbeh for body and bitterness, s'chug for heat. How much of each you add is personal.

It is usually eaten with bread for tearing and dipping. Time Out's 2018 review of one Kerem restaurant lists **lahoh**, the soft, spongy pancake-like bread, and **saluf**, a Yemenite flatbread, as coming with every main course. Beyond the classic beef or chicken soup, the same review mentions **calf's-foot soup** and **oxtail soup**, alongside rice and beans and spicy meatballs.

None of this is fine dining, and it is not meant to be. The Times of Israel called the meal simple and unfussy, and promised that the flavour more than makes up for it.

## Shimon and the soup houses behind Akiva Street

The name that comes up most in writing about Kerem soup is **Shimon**. The two sources we use locate a restaurant of that name in slightly different terms, and we cannot confirm from them whether they describe the same establishment or the same address.

The **Times of Israel** (2012) names two soup restaurants, **Yisrael's** and **Shimon's**, which it says face each other behind Akiva Street and are both run by local Yemenite families. It recommends either for the soup. Rabbi Akiva Street runs along the edge of the market, and the lanes behind it lead into the Kerem's grid.

**Time Out Israel**, in a 2018 listing titled [Shimon, The King of Soups](https://www.timeout.com/israel/restaurants/shimon-the-king-of-soups), places a restaurant called "Shimon, The King of Soups" at **28 Yehya Kapah Street**, in the Yemenite Quarter, which the review says "hugs" Carmel Market. It describes a barebones dive run by **Yonit Saada**, daughter of the late Shimon, and credits it with some of the best Yemenite food in the country. The dishes listed are those mentioned above: calf's-foot soup, oxtail soup, rice and beans, spicy meatballs, with hilbeh, s'chug, lahoh and saluf served alongside. Saada is quoted on her method: "I have a basic recipe but I cook with my eyes."

Yehya Kapah Street is named after Rabbi Yihye Qafih, the Sana'a scholar who supported himself as a goldsmith; his story is told in our piece on [who the streets of the Kerem are named for](/blog/kerem-hateimanim-street-names-yemenite-history). On the map it runs through the middle of the quarter, a couple of blocks from the market.

We have not established whether the 2012 and 2018 descriptions point to one door or two, or whether either is still there. Treat both names as leads to check, not a confirmed itinerary.

## Hummus and the other tables of the quarter

Soup is the Kerem's speciality, but it is not the only thing on offer. The 2012 Times of Israel article also names **Zion** and **Medina** as two of the neighbourhood's most famous eateries, without describing their menus. Of the Kerem's restaurants in general, it says they serve everything from grilled meat and salads to hummus and dessert.

In 2016, [Time Out Israel](https://www.timeout.com/israel/blog/yemenite-spice-a-guide-to-kerem-hateimanim-tel-avivs-latest-lgbtq-hotspot-110416) published a short guide to the quarter by Kristof Steiner that adds several more. It describes **Shlomo & Doron Hummus**, at 29 Yishkon Street, as known for its spicy hummus; **Anat's Kitchen**, where the owner herself served home-cooked lunches, with no street address given; and **Café Yom Tov**, at 30 Yom Tov Street, a shabby, randomly furnished café for morning coffee and small plates in the evening. Of the neighbourhood's eateries as a whole, the guide says they are authentic and traditional.

That article is now ten years old; we mention these places as it described them, without having checked that they are still there.

## When to go

We are not giving opening hours, because the sources do not give them and they change. The patterns below are general: reasons to check in advance, not rules.

**Lunch is the main event.** Soup houses and hummus counters of this kind traditionally do most of their business in the middle of the day. Arriving mid-morning or late afternoon risks finding the pot empty or the shutter half down.

**Weekdays may be easier.** In Tel Aviv generally, markets and the streets around them tend to be busiest towards the end of the week, so a weekday is likely to be calmer. Our sources say nothing about crowds in the Kerem itself.

**Friday may be short.** Across Tel Aviv, many businesses wind down on Friday afternoon ahead of Shabbat. Our sources do not say whether the Kerem's restaurants follow that pattern, so if you want lunch on a Friday, check first and go early.

**In Tel Aviv, Shabbat often means closures — check each address.** Many businesses in the city close from Friday afternoon until Saturday evening. None of our sources says whether the restaurants named here do, so do not assume either way. If you are visiting over a weekend, our guide to [Tel Aviv on Shabbat](/blog/tel-aviv-on-shabbat-what-is-open-getting-around) explains what tends to stay open and how to get around.

The practical rule is simple: look up any restaurant you want to visit — a recent map listing, a phone call, or a question to a neighbour — on the day you plan to go.

## A walk from the market into the Kerem

The simplest way to eat here is to arrive on foot from Carmel Market and let the quarter come to you.

What follows is a suggested route of our own, not something taken from the sources. You might enter the market from Magen David Square or from the side lanes and walk down towards its southern end. From there, turn into one of the lanes off Rabbi Akiva Street on the sea side of the market. Within a few steps the noise should drop and the buildings get lower, and you will be in the Kerem's grid of narrow streets.

Take your time. The quarter is small enough that you can walk its length in a few minutes, and the restaurants are easy to miss — a hand-painted sign, a doorway with a few plastic chairs, a smell of broth. Yehya Kapah Street crosses the middle of the grid; Yishkon and Yom Tov Streets run nearby. Have the names you want to try written down, and check them against a current map, because the sources above are not.

After lunch, the beach is a short walk west. The Times of Israel put the Mediterranean about three blocks away, and that is roughly how it feels.

## Staying close to the soup

Most visitors eat in the Kerem once, on a day trip to the market. Staying in the quarter changes that: a soup lunch becomes something you can do on any weekday.
